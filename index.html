THE GAME

A sport built on momentum

Two teams try to shoot a hard rubber disc — the puck — into the opposing net more times than their opponent, while skating at speeds that can top 20 miles an hour.

What sets hockey apart from most team sports is that play almost never stops. Substitutions happen on the fly, without a whistle, as tired players skate to the bench and fresh ones jump over the boards mid-play. That constant motion is why a single shift — a player's stretch of ice time — usually lasts less than a minute before legs give out.

The sport is also unusually physical for one played on such a slippery surface. Body checking, where a defender uses their hips or shoulder to separate an opponent from the puck, is a legal and central part of the men's game, while stick skill and positioning matter more in women's and youth hockey, where checking is restricted or removed.

ORIGINS

How the game took shape

Hockey's roots stretch back further than most fans realise, drawing on stick-and-ball games played across Europe long before skates ever touched a frozen pond.

1800s

Field games like hurling and bandy, played in the British Isles and Northern Europe, were adapted onto ice by soldiers and settlers in Canada, who added skates and a flatter puck to stop the ball from bouncing on rough ice.

1875

The first organised indoor game with written rules was played at the Victoria Skating Rink in Montreal, generally treated as the birth of modern hockey as a codified sport rather than an informal pastime.

1893

Canada's Governor General, Lord Stanley of Preston, donated a silver bowl as a challenge trophy for the country's top amateur club team — the Stanley Cup, still the oldest trophy competed for by professional athletes in North America.

1917

The National Hockey League was founded in Montreal with four Canadian teams, eventually growing into the dominant professional league and expanding into the United States through the 1920s.

1920

Ice hockey made its debut as an Olympic sport at the Antwerp Summer Games, before moving permanently to the Winter Olympics from 1924 onward.

1990

The first Women's World Championship was held in Ottawa, accelerating the growth of organised women's hockey, which became a full Olympic medal event in 1998.

STRUCTURE

The rink, the periods, the whistle

A regulation rink is divided by five lines that govern almost every rule in the sport. The two blue lines split the ice into three zones — defensive, neutral, and attacking — and are the basis for hockey's best-known rule, offside: an attacking player cannot cross the blue line into the attacking zone before the puck does.

The red centre line matters for icing: if a team shoots the puck from behind its own line all the way down the ice untouched, play stops and it's brought back for a faceoff in their own zone. Two thin red goal lines mark where the net sits — cross that line with the puck, and it's a goal.

Games are split into three periods, with the clock counting down and stopping on every whistle — which is why an "hour-long" game usually takes closer to two and a half hours to finish.

Penalties add another layer: a player sent to the box for two minutes leaves their team short-handed, giving the opponent a power play. Common penalties include tripping, slashing, and roughing — each judged by two on-ice officials who skate the full length of the rink for the entire game.

THE TEAM

Six roles, one shift

Only six players from each team are on the ice at once, and each has a distinct job that shifts constantly as the puck changes hands.

Goaltender
1 on ice
The only player allowed to catch the puck or drop to block it directly, wearing padding roughly twice the bulk of any teammate. A goaltender's save percentage — the share of shots stopped — is the single most-watched statistic in the sport.
Defence
2 on ice
Paired as a left and right defenceman, their main job is stopping attacks in their own zone and starting new ones by passing the puck up to forwards — though many modern defencemen join the rush and score as often as forwards did a generation ago.
Wingers
2 on ice
Skate the outer lanes of the ice, generally staying on their strong side, and are usually a team's primary scorers, positioned to finish passes from the centre near the net.
Centre
1 on ice
Plays the middle of the ice, takes most faceoffs, and is expected to cover the most ground of any position — dropping back to help on defence, then leading the rush forward.
GEAR

What players wear, and why

Equipment in hockey isn't cosmetic — every piece exists to absorb a specific kind of impact, from a 100-mile-an-hour shot to a full-speed collision along the boards.

Skates

Blades are ground into a slight curve, or "rocker," so players can pivot on a single point of contact rather than the full length of the blade.

Stick

Modern sticks are made from layered carbon fibre, not wood, and are chosen for a specific flex rating matched to a player's strength and shot style.

Helmet & visor

Mandatory at every level; professional leagues debate full face shields versus half visors, balancing protection against peripheral vision.

Shoulder & elbow pads

Hard plastic shells over foam padding absorb checks along the boards, one of the most common points of contact in the game.

Goaltender pads

Leg pads alone can weigh several kilograms each, built wide and flat to cover as much of the net as possible when a goaltender drops down.

The puck

Frozen before games to keep it from bouncing, a vulcanized rubber disc just one inch thick that can leave the stick faster than most cars accelerate.

Where the game is played

Hockey's biggest stage runs from professional arenas in North America and Europe to frozen ponds and driveways wherever winters get cold enough — and increasingly, on artificial ice in warm-weather cities too.

National Hockey League (NHL)
The top professional men's league, made up of teams across the United States and Canada, culminating each spring in the playoffs for the Stanley Cup.
IIHF World Championship
An annual international tournament run by the sport's global governing body, separate from the Olympics and open to national teams from more than 50 countries.
Winter Olympics
Held every four years, featuring both men's and women's tournaments and historically some of the sport's most-watched games, including several famous upsets.
Women's leagues
The Professional Women's Hockey League (PWHL) launched in North America in 2024, joining established European and collegiate women's competitions.
Youth & recreational hockey
Played on modified rules — often with no checking — through minor leagues, school programs, and casual pond and street hockey games worldwide.
