<section class="intro" id="intro">
  <div class="wrap intro-grid">
    <div>
      <div class="section-head">
        <span class="tag">THE GAME</span>
        <h2>A sport built on momentum</h2>
      </div>
      <p class="lede">Two teams try to shoot a hard rubber disc — the puck — into the opposing net more times than their opponent, while skating at speeds that can top 20 miles an hour.</p>
      <p class="body-text">What sets hockey apart from most team sports is that play almost never stops. Substitutions happen on the fly, without a whistle, as tired players skate to the bench and fresh ones jump over the boards mid-play. That constant motion is why a single shift — a player's stretch of ice time — usually lasts less than a minute before legs give out.</p>
      <p class="body-text">The sport is also unusually physical for one played on such a slippery surface. Body checking, where a defender uses their hips or shoulder to separate an opponent from the puck, is a legal and central part of the men's game, while stick skill and positioning matter more in women's and youth hockey, where checking is restricted or removed.</p>
    </div>
    <div>
      <img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1551241784-1058bb555220?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1000&q=80" alt="A hockey player controlling the puck with their stick close to the boards" style="border-radius:4px; width:100%; height:520px; object-fit:cover;">
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="history" id="history">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="tag" style="color:#ff8a94;">ORIGINS</span>
      <h2>How the game took shape</h2>
      <p class="lede">Hockey's roots stretch back further than most fans realise, drawing on stick-and-ball games played across Europe long before skates ever touched a frozen pond.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="timeline">
      <div class="t-item">
        <div class="t-year">1800s</div>
        <p>Field games like hurling and bandy, played in the British Isles and Northern Europe, were adapted onto ice by soldiers and settlers in Canada, who added skates and a flatter puck to stop the ball from bouncing on rough ice.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="t-item">
        <div class="t-year">1875</div>
        <p>The first organised indoor game with written rules was played at the Victoria Skating Rink in Montreal, generally treated as the birth of modern hockey as a codified sport rather than an informal pastime.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="t-item">
        <div class="t-year">1893</div>
        <p>Canada's Governor General, Lord Stanley of Preston, donated a silver bowl as a challenge trophy for the country's top amateur club team — the Stanley Cup, still the oldest trophy competed for by professional athletes in North America.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="t-item">
        <div class="t-year">1917</div>
        <p>The National Hockey League was founded in Montreal with four Canadian teams, eventually growing into the dominant professional league and expanding into the United States through the 1920s.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="t-item">
        <div class="t-year">1920</div>
        <p>Ice hockey made its debut as an Olympic sport at the Antwerp Summer Games, before moving permanently to the Winter Olympics from 1924 onward.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="t-item">
        <div class="t-year">1990</div>
        <p>The first Women's World Championship was held in Ottawa, accelerating the growth of organised women's hockey, which became a full Olympic medal event in 1998.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="rink" id="rink">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="tag">STRUCTURE</span>
      <h2>The rink, the periods, the whistle</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="rink-layout">
      <div>
        <p class="body-text">A regulation rink is divided by five lines that govern almost every rule in the sport. The two blue lines split the ice into three zones — defensive, neutral, and attacking — and are the basis for hockey's best-known rule, offside: an attacking player cannot cross the blue line into the attacking zone before the puck does.</p>
        <p class="body-text" style="margin-top:14px;">The red centre line matters for icing: if a team shoots the puck from behind its own line all the way down the ice untouched, play stops and it's brought back for a faceoff in their own zone. Two thin red goal lines mark where the net sits — cross that line with the puck, and it's a goal.</p>

        </div>
      </div>
      <div>
        <p class="body-text">Games are split into three periods, with the clock counting down and stopping on every whistle — which is why an "hour-long" game usually takes closer to two and a half hours to finish.</p>
        <div class="periods">
          <div>
            <div class="p-num">1st</div>
            <div class="p-desc">20 minutes. Teams switch ends after the period.</div>
          </div>
          <div>
            <div class="p-num">2nd</div>
            <div class="p-desc">20 minutes. Teams switch ends again.</div>
          </div>
          <div>
            <div class="p-num">3rd</div>
            <div class="p-desc">20 minutes. Decides the winner, or forces overtime.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <p class="body-text" style="margin-top:34px;">Penalties add another layer: a player sent to the box for two minutes leaves their team short-handed, giving the opponent a power play. Common penalties include tripping, slashing, and roughing — each judged by two on-ice officials who skate the full length of the rink for the entire game.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="positions" id="positions">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="tag" style="color:#ff8a94;">THE TEAM</span>
      <h2>Six roles, one shift</h2>
      <p class="lede">Only six players from each team are on the ice at once, and each has a distinct job that shifts constantly as the puck changes hands.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="pos-list">
      <div class="pos-row">
        <div>
          <div class="p-name">Goaltender</div>
          <span class="p-count">1 on ice</span>
        </div>
        <div class="p-detail">The only player allowed to catch the puck or drop to block it directly, wearing padding roughly twice the bulk of any teammate. A goaltender's save percentage — the share of shots stopped — is the single most-watched statistic in the sport.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="pos-row">
        <div>
          <div class="p-name">Defence</div>
          <span class="p-count">2 on ice</span>
        </div>
        <div class="p-detail">Paired as a left and right defenceman, their main job is stopping attacks in their own zone and starting new ones by passing the puck up to forwards — though many modern defencemen join the rush and score as often as forwards did a generation ago.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="pos-row">
        <div>
          <div class="p-name">Wingers</div>
          <span class="p-count">2 on ice</span>
        </div>
        <div class="p-detail">Skate the outer lanes of the ice, generally staying on their strong side, and are usually a team's primary scorers, positioned to finish passes from the centre near the net.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="pos-row">
        <div>
          <div class="p-name">Centre</div>
          <span class="p-count">1 on ice</span>
        </div>
        <div class="p-detail">Plays the middle of the ice, takes most faceoffs, and is expected to cover the most ground of any position — dropping back to help on defence, then leading the rush forward.</div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="equipment" id="equipment">
  <div class="wrap">
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="tag">GEAR</span>
      <h2>What players wear, and why</h2>
      <p class="lede">Equipment in hockey isn't cosmetic — every piece exists to absorb a specific kind of impact, from a 100-mile-an-hour shot to a full-speed collision along the boards.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="equip-grid">
      <div class="equip-item">
        <h3>Skates</h3>
        <p>Blades are ground into a slight curve, or "rocker," so players can pivot on a single point of contact rather than the full length of the blade.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="equip-item">
        <h3>Stick</h3>
        <p>Modern sticks are made from layered carbon fibre, not wood, and are chosen for a specific flex rating matched to a player's strength and shot style.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="equip-item">
        <h3>Helmet &amp; visor</h3>
        <p>Mandatory at every level; professional leagues debate full face shields versus half visors, balancing protection against peripheral vision.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="equip-item">
        <h3>Shoulder &amp; elbow pads</h3>
        <p>Hard plastic shells over foam padding absorb checks along the boards, one of the most common points of contact in the game.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="equip-item">
        <h3>Goaltender pads</h3>
        <p>Leg pads alone can weigh several kilograms each, built wide and flat to cover as much of the net as possible when a goaltender drops down.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="equip-item">
        <h3>The puck</h3>
        <p>Frozen before games to keep it from bouncing, a vulcanized rubber disc just one inch thick that can leave the stick faster than most cars accelerate.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="where" id="where">
  <div class="wrap where-grid">
    <div>
      <div class="section-head">
        <span class="tag">TODAY</span>
        <h2>Where the game is played</h2>
      </div>
      <p class="body-text">Hockey's biggest stage runs from professional arenas in North America and Europe to frozen ponds and driveways wherever winters get cold enough — and increasingly, on artificial ice in warm-weather cities too.</p>
    </div>
    <dl class="where-list">
      <dt>National Hockey League (NHL)</dt>
      <dd>The top professional men's league, made up of teams across the United States and Canada, culminating each spring in the playoffs for the Stanley Cup.</dd>
      <dt>IIHF World Championship</dt>
      <dd>An annual international tournament run by the sport's global governing body, separate from the Olympics and open to national teams from more than 50 countries.</dd>
      <dt>Winter Olympics</dt>
      <dd>Held every four years, featuring both men's and women's tournaments and historically some of the sport's most-watched games, including several famous upsets.</dd>
      <dt>Women's leagues</dt>
      <dd>The Professional Women's Hockey League (PWHL) launched in North America in 2024, joining established European and collegiate women's competitions.</dd>
      <dt>Youth &amp; recreational hockey</dt>
      <dd>Played on modified rules — often with no checking — through minor leagues, school programs, and casual pond and street hockey games worldwide.</dd>
    </dl>
  </div>
</section>
